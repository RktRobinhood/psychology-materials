How to organize your notes: Use the six unit names as top-level sections. Each unit combines psychological content, a real-world context and recurring concepts. The syllabus checklist lists every required term and learning objective.

This is a teaching sequence, not a fixed calendar. Dates and assessment deadlines are announced separately. Course choices may be refined without changing the official syllabus requirements.

The IB Psychology framework

How the syllabus fits together

The same psychological idea often appears in several units. That overlap is intentional.

Content

Psychological terminology and explanations from the biological, cognitive and sociocultural approaches, plus research methodology.

Official Tables 3.1–3.4

Contexts

Apply what you know to learning and cognition, human relationships, health and well-being, and human development.

Official Tables 4.1–4.13

Concepts

Use bias, causality, change, measurement, perspective and responsibility to evaluate explanations and research.

Used throughout all six units

Research practicals in our sequence

  1. After Unit 3ExperimentTrue or quasi-experiment. Minimum sample: 5 participants repeated measures OR 10 participants independent measures.
  2. After Unit 4Survey / questionnaireMinimum sample: 10 participants.
  3. After Unit 5InterviewStructured, semi-structured or focus group; minimum one individual interview or 3–8 people in one focus group.
  4. After Unit 6ObservationNaturalistic or controlled, overt/covert, participant/non-participant; minimum one participant.

Assessment

  • Paper 1: explain content, apply it to unseen situations, and write concept-led analysis.
  • Paper 2: explain and compare research methods using class practicals; analyse an unseen study using concepts.
  • IA: a research proposal, not a report of an experiment you have already carried out.

Higher Level

  • Culture, motivation and technology across the four contexts.
  • Data analysis and interpretation, examined in HL Paper 3.
  • SL also studies data analysis, but it is not separately assessed at SL.

The guide lists all four practicals as compulsory. Their allocation to particular contexts is recommended by the IB, not mandatory; the order above is our course plan.

Year 1 · 1i · Unit 01

Psychology as a Way of Thinking

How do psychologists develop, test and evaluate explanations of behaviour?

Foundations across all four contextsGuide: Concepts · Table 3.4
Lessons for this unit →

Core ideas

  • The six recurring concepts: bias, causality, change, measurement, perspective, responsibility.
  • The biological, cognitive and sociocultural approaches, and how they overlap.
  • The difference between psychological theories, models, research methods and evidence.

Research foundations

  • Ethical decision-making, informed consent and the right to withdraw.
  • How studies support or challenge claims; correlation versus causation.
  • Introducing sampling, research bias, validity, reliability and method evaluation.

Your notes should cover

  • Key terms and what they mean, not definitions alone.
  • Theories/models: how they work, what they explain and their limitations.
  • At least one appropriate example of research or an applied situation.
  • How to connect the unit to concepts and research methods.

Milgram, the Stanford Prison Experiment, Little Albert and the Marshmallow Test are classroom examples, not prescribed IB case studies.

Year 1 · 1i · Unit 03

Memory, Models and the Brain

How can we explain memory, investigate the brain and improve cognition?

Learning and cognition · cognitive processesGuide: Tables 3.1, 3.2, 4.12, 4.13
Lessons for this unit →

Cognitive models

  • Memory as the selected cognitive process.
  • The Multi-Store Model (MSM) and Working Memory Model (WMM).
  • What models explain, how evidence tests them, and their strengths and limitations.

Factors affecting memory

  • Biological factors: localization of function, imaging and neuroplasticity.
  • Cultural and environmental influences on cognition.
  • Evidence-informed strategies for improving a cognitive process.

  • Key terms and what they mean, not definitions alone.
  • Theories/models: how they work, what they explain and their limitations.

Experiment practical
After the Learning and Cognition context

Small groups run a safe experiment, collect data, critique methods and share findings. This supports Paper 2; the 2027 IA is a separate research proposal.

Teaching examples may include H.M., Clive Wearing, Maguire, Draganski, Rosenzweig/Bennett & Diamond, and Ramachandran. No particular study is compulsory.

Year 2 · 2i · Unit 05

Mental Health, Behaviour Change and Culture

How are mental health and health problems explained, addressed and prevented?

Health and well-beingGuide: Tables 3.1, 3.3, 4.1–4.4
Lessons for this unit →

Mental health disorder

  • Working course choice: major depressive disorder (MDD).
  • Biological explanations; cognitive models; environmental factors.
  • Cultural differences in prevalence and mental health approaches.

Health problem and intervention

  • Working course choice: social media addiction.
  • Changes and differences in prevalence; social learning; stress.
  • Biological and psychological treatments for the disorder; prevention or treatment for the health problem.

Useful cross-links

  • Biological reductionism; neurotransmission.
  • Cognitive dissonance; emic and etic approaches.

Interview practical
After the Health and Well-being context

Use structured/semi-structured interviews or a focus group on a safe, non-sensitive health topic; consider ethics and qualitative interpretation.

MDD and social media addiction are working course choices. The guide offers other eligible disorders and health problems; it does not require every option.

Human developmentGuide: Tables 3.1, 3.3, 4.5–4.7

Development of self

Biological foundations

  • Animal research/models and ethics.

Observation practical
After the Human Development context
