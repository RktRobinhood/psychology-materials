### Section A (4 marks each)

- The question always names a **content point, not a study**, e.g. "describe cognitive load theory" [59705].
- **Command term: describe or explain** [70442, 64581]:
  - *Describe* = the "what": the characteristics or mechanisms of the theory.
  - *Explain* = the "why/how": why the behaviour happens, what each mechanism does. Explaining also means linking the example back to the content [70442].
- **Two strands** [64925]:
  1. Describe or explain the content point.
  2. Give **one example** and make the link to the content point explicit.
- The example may be a **study or an anecdotal (everyday) example**. "Anecdotal" means not a published study; it does not have to be personal [59705, 68593].
- With a study, give a brief outline (aim, rough procedure, findings) and then **link it**. Full procedural detail is not needed, and the study earns only about 2 marks [68593].
- **Only the first example is marked.** Extra examples waste time [64925, 65434, 72284].
- Recommended layout: **two paragraphs**, the content point first, then the example and its link [64925, 65434].
- Even if a question does not ask for an example, give one [72109].
- The paper is marked on the validity of the example, not the accuracy of study details. Saying "bell" instead of "metronome" for Pavlov would not lose marks [65434].

### Section B (6 marks each)

- The stem is an **unseen scenario** (stimulus), usually a short vignette of 3 to 8 sentences about a named person or group. It is set in one of the four contexts, and the question uses terminology from the approach tables [59706].
- The question is almost always **"Explain how X could be used to explain / change ..."** [c59706: John "cannot imagine a different command term"].
- **Two strands** [71976]:
  1. Knowledge of the relevant theory or content.
  2. Linking it to the scenario with correct terminology.
- Typical stimulus shapes seen on the site:
  - a character with a problem behaviour, asking how to change it with OC, CC or SLT [65394, 52681, 64601, 50766]
  - a character whose behaviour needs explaining with schema theory, cognitive load or WMM [67166, 66860, 61882]
  - a mini research study, asking about the value of a model or animal research [59706; IB specimen in c59706]
- **IB specimen question** (paraphrased from c59706): researchers propose a three-stage model of imagination (select elements, combine them randomly, evaluate the output) and watch primary children build imaginary creatures from toy body parts. Task: with reference to the study, **explain the value of cognitive models for understanding cognitive processes [6]**.
  - Markscheme points include: models are tested empirically; one study rarely tests a whole model; constructs must be operationalised; the study supports some stages but not the claim that combination is random.
  - John: knowing MSM/WMM plus *why psychologists use models* is enough [c59706].
- **Top-answer habits** [71976, 67166, 67655]:
  - Open with a short explanation of the theory.
  - Pick 2 to 3 details from the scenario and write "this happens **because** ..." rather than just labelling.
  - A few well-developed links beat many shallow ones.
  - Paraphrase scenario details; don't copy long quotations.
  - **No evaluation.**
  - Self-test: if you could have written it without reading the scenario, there is too little application.
  - John's own students write one paragraph and no conclusion [c59706].
- Unofficial markscheme caps [67166]:
  - theory only, with no reference to the scenario: **maximum 3**
  - scenario only, with no theory: **maximum 2**

### Section C (15 marks)

- **Choose one of two concept-based extended responses**, each from a different context [59707].
- Every question has **a concept + an area of study / learning objective + a context** [59707, blog 71936], e.g. "Discuss bias with regard to ...".
- **Command terms**: most will be **discuss**, but **evaluate, to what extent and examine** are possible. The IB specimen used "evaluate". "To what extent"/"evaluate" fits perspective best. "Compare and contrast" is unlikely [c69782, Laura and John, Sept 2026].
- **Knowing details of studies is not assessed**, and no research is required; the concept must be the focus [59707, c59707].
- **Three strands, best-fit, single 15-mark band** [67173, 67141, 59707]:
  1. **Knowledge and understanding** of the area of study AND the concept.
  2. **Critical analysis**: links between area of study and concept made *throughout* and explained; a reasoned conclusion consistent with the argument.
  3. **Terminology** that is accurate and precise; points relevant, accurate and detailed.

**Markbands** [67173, 67141] (paraphrased):

| Marks | Understanding | Analysis | Conclusion | Terminology |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | little grasp of the question; very limited knowledge with errors | descriptive or superficial; links missing or irrelevant | superficial or inconsistent | absent or misused |
| 4–6 | some understanding; knowledge described | mostly descriptive; links stated, partly relevant | simplistic | often misused; points vague |
| 7–9 | demands only partly met; knowledge partly explained | undeveloped; links explained, partly relevant | not always consistent | sometimes appropriate; points lack accuracy and development |
| 10–12 | demands addressed; knowledge mostly explained | critical but underdeveloped; links included and explained | consistent | mostly appropriate; points accurate but lack detail |
| 13–15 | knowledge fully explained | well-developed critical analysis; links throughout, fully explained | reasoned, clearly stated, consistent | accurate and precise; points detailed |

**Best-fit worked example** [59707]: strands at 10–12, 7–9 and 4–6 give about 8 marks overall.

**Essay scaffolds** from the "Essay type" pages. These are suggestions only, and two of the three strands is enough:
- **Bias**, SPR [66756]: Sampling, Participant, Researcher bias.
- **Causality**, 3 Ms [66220]: Methodology (is a true experiment possible?), Moderating variables, Measurement.
- **Change**, MIT [66757]: Mechanisms, Impact, Time (longitudinal designs, temporal validity).
- **Measurement**, CTC [66753]: Constructs, Tools, Context/culture.
- **Perspective** [65928]: intro, describe the model, perspective 1 (e.g. cognitive: assumptions or support), brief evaluation *of the perspective*, perspective 2 (e.g. biological, HM), then a conclusion on why one lens is not enough.
- **Responsibility**, AEAC [66758]: Agency, Ethics, Application (avoid stigma, determinism, deficit framing), Cultural awareness.

**Sentence stems** for the concepts [65934, 65931]. Avoid "internal validity was high", "causality was low", "the study was unethical" or "the study was biased". Instead write "internal validity is strengthened/weakened by ...", "a causal claim is supported/limited by ...", and so on.

### What a top answer does

**Section A** [64925, 65434, 72285, 71997, 72119, 72120]:
- Paragraph 1 states the theory using its own vocabulary (NS/UCS/CS/CR; the four reinforcement/punishment terms; ARRM plus vicarious reinforcement; assimilation/accommodation).
- For "explain", say *why* it happens.
- Paragraph 2 gives one example and maps each term onto it.
- A 4/4 SLT answer names attention, retention, reproduction and motivation, *and* shows vicarious reinforcement and identification inside the Bandura example. A 2/4 answer only says "people copy others" [71997].
- A 4/4 model answer gives three MSM stores with capacity, duration and encoding, plus rehearsal and displacement, then HM as evidence that STM and LTM are separate [72285].

**Section B** [65394, 67166, 66860]:
- Define the theory in one or two lines, then apply each part to named scenario details.
- For change questions, justify the choice. Example: choose a fixed-ratio schedule (a homework pass after five on-time arrivals) and explain why that beats rewarding every arrival [65394].
- For conditioning, map UCS/NS/CS onto the scenario (e.g. favourite music = UCS, reading = NS → CS) [65394].
- The 6/6 cognitive-load answer shows how the loads *interact*: high extraneous load leaves less capacity for germane processing, so recall is poor [66860].

**Section C** [67173, 67304, 67141, 65928, blog 71936]:
- Keep the area of study present all the way through. An essay that only discusses bias, with no actual effect of technology on memory, was capped at about 9 [67141].
- Link the concept to the content in every paragraph, not as an add-on.
- Weigh the perspectives against each other.
- Unpack every term.
- End with a conclusion that follows from the argument and adds nothing new.
- A 13/15 MSM-perspective essay [67173 sample 2]:
  - described the MSM in detail
  - used Glanzer and Cunitz for the cognitive lens and HM for the biological lens
  - noted that cognitive processes can only be inferred
  - concluded that the cognitive view explains *what/how* and the biological view the *mechanism*
  - lost marks for dwelling on study methodology instead of the perspective, and for not using HM's procedural memory as a *challenge* to the model

## 3. TOPICS

### 3.0 The cognitive approach (framing)

- **Assumptions** [50725]:
  - Humans are information processors (input, processing, output; bottom-up sensory input combined with top-down stored knowledge).
  - Mental processes can be studied scientifically.
  - Mental representations guide behaviour.
  - Soft determinism: change your thinking and you can change your behaviour.
- The approach arose in the 1950s in reaction to behaviourism. Skinner treated the mind as a "black box" [50725, 55781].
- Neisser criticised lab tasks as artificial, which is why cognitive research now also happens in natural settings [50725].
- **Cognitive miser** (Fiske and Taylor, 1991): we skip effortful processing because of limited knowledge, low motivation or economy (time, money) [50725, 52682].
- **Why psychologists use models** [52683]:
  - to simplify processes that can't be seen
  - to break them into testable mechanisms
  - to predict behaviour
  - to communicate ideas visually
  - to let researchers test one component at a time
- **Limits of models** [52683]:
  - oversimplified (the MSM ignores emotion and distortion)
  - built on assumptions about storage and linear flow
  - hypothetical constructs that are hard to measure
  - unclear directionality
  - Drawing a model alone never earns full marks; describe its components and how they interact [52683].

### 3.1 The six concepts

**Bias** [55777, 62665, 64652]:
- Systematic error in thinking, research or practice that threatens validity and reliability, often unintended.
- **Bias vs perspective**: bias is unconscious and distorting; perspective is a deliberate lens that adds depth [55777].
  - Analogy [64652]: perspective = the glasses you wear; bias = a smudge on the glasses.
  - Using one approach (e.g. biological) is a *perspective*, not a bias [55777].
- Types:
  - participant: expectancy effect, demand characteristics, social desirability
  - researcher: confirmation, interviewer
  - sampling/selection, attrition
  - cultural: etic lens, ecological fallacy, WEIRD samples
  - gender: alpha bias exaggerates sex differences, beta bias minimises them
  - publication bias, p-hacking
  - recall, optimism, peak-end rule [55777]
- Controls: single/double blind; random sampling and allocation; cross-cultural and emic research; researcher triangulation [55777].
- L&C concept-wall terms: cultural bias, measurement bias, recall bias, self-report bias, WEIRD sampling [62665].
- L&C research often samples university students or very young children; 8–15-year-olds are rarely studied [65637].

**Causality** [50714, 63508]:
- "Correlation is not causation" is an oversimplification; correlation is a *hint*.
- **Linear causality**: an IV is manipulated → effect on the DV under control; cause precedes effect; the effect traces back to one cause.
- **Domino causality**: each effect becomes the next cause, possibly branching. Examples: stress → cortisol → MAO-A → serotonin; Meaney's rats, where neglect → reduced GR gene expression → high cortisol → hippocampal cell death → poor spatial memory.
- **Cyclical causality**: feedback loops, e.g. leptin/ghrelin in hunger.
- **Bradford Hill criteria** strengthen a causal case from correlational data: strength/effect size, consistency, specificity, temporality, dose-response, plausibility, supporting experimental evidence [50714].
- L&C terms: bidirectional ambiguity, counterbalancing, internal validity, randomised controlled experiments [63508].

**Change** [55779, 64379]:
- Covers the free will vs determinism debate and **agency**: intentionality, self-regulation, self-efficacy, responsibility.
- **Locus of control**: internal vs external; Rotter's 29-item scale.
- Kinds of determinism: biological, environmental, and **soft determinism**, the middle ground that the cognitive approach takes.
- Barriers to change: internal (habit, fear, low self-efficacy) and external (resources, norms, environment).
- L&C terms: agency and potential for change, environmental determinism, self-efficacy, soft determinism [64379].

**Measurement** [55780, 64378]:
- Objective vs subjective data; anecdotal vs empirical data.
- Quantitative data needs **operationalisation** of constructs; qualitative data needs researcher **reflexivity**.
- Self-report weaknesses: lack of insight, overconfidence, social desirability, retrospective distortion from reconstructive memory.
- Remedy: **triangulation** (method, data, researcher).
- L&C terms: brain-imaging artefacts, voxel-based morphometry, isolating variables, the link between measurement and internal validity, researcher triangulation [64378].

**Perspective** [55781, 64380]:
- No single perspective explains behaviour. Named perspectives: biological, cognitive, behavioural, psychodynamic, humanistic, sociocultural, evolutionary, ecological, cross-cultural.
- **Positivism** (Skinner): objective, quantitative, deductive, reductionist, seeks general laws.
- **Interpretivism** (Freud): subjective, qualitative, inductive, holistic.
- Indigenous perspectives see physical, spiritual, emotional and mental life as interconnected [55781].
- L&C terms: action research; biological, cognitive and sociocultural approaches; Indigenous perspective [64380].

**Responsibility** [55782, 64381]:
- Research ethics: avoid harm, confidentiality, informed consent, right to withdraw, deception only when essential, debrief.
- Case-study risks: objectification, hard to withdraw.
- Socially sensitive research, cultural competence, avoid pathologising Indigenous beliefs.
- Duties to clients: competence, duty of care, autonomy.
- L&C terms: don't report selectively; learning research often uses children, older adults or clinical groups; present findings cautiously; cognitive tests can reinforce stereotypes [64381].

### 3.2 Classical conditioning (Pavlov)

**Definition** [52680, 68691]: learning by association.
- A **neutral stimulus (NS)** is repeatedly paired with an **unconditioned stimulus (UCS)** that triggers a reflex (**unconditioned response, UCR**).
- The NS becomes a **conditioned stimulus (CS)** that produces a **conditioned response (CR)** on its own.
- **Extinction**: the CR fades if the CS keeps appearing without the UCS.

**Pavlov (1903)** [68691, 65434, 52680]:
- Pavlov was a physiologist studying digestion in dogs. He noticed the dogs salivated at the sight of the lab assistants who fed them (a chance observation).
- Procedure: food (UCS) → salivation (UCR). A metronome (NS) was paired with food repeatedly, until the metronome alone (CS) produced salivation (CR).
- Conclusion: behaviour can be learned when an environmental stimulus becomes associated with a biologically significant one. This was the foundation of behaviourism.
- Concept links [68691]:
  - *causality*: tight lab control isolates the pairing as the cause
  - *measurement*: salivation is an objective, quantifiable response, but inner states are ignored
  - *perspective*: behaviourist and reductionist; assumes dog learning generalises to humans (animal model)

**Other studies and applications** [52680, 61243, 64565]:
- **Thompson (1983)**: rabbit eye-blink conditioning. The cerebellum (interpositus nucleus) is essential for the CR. Ethics were debated.
- **Garcia (1955)**: rats drank saccharin during radiation. They avoided saccharin for over a month after a single pairing (taste aversion).
- **Aversion therapy**: an emetic drug paired with alcohol → nausea associated with alcohol.
  - Responsibility issue: aversion methods were misused as "conversion therapy", first in Czechoslovakia in 1951 and in the UK in 1961. Today the APA and BPS oppose it.
  - Responsibility worksheet: **Watson and Rayner (1920)** (Little Albert) and **Meyer and Chesser (1970)** (aversion therapy with apomorphine; some relapse) [61243].
- Contexts: fear extinction for PTSD; aversion to stop smoking; crowd management [52680].
- What the examiner wants: correct terminology, with the NS, UCS and CS mapped onto the example [52680].

### 3.3 Operant conditioning (Thorndike, Skinner)

**Definition** [52681]: behaviour changes according to its consequences. It rests on **Thorndike's Law of Effect** (pleasant consequences make a behaviour more likely to recur, unpleasant ones less likely). Skinner extended it.

**Key terms** [52681, 61243]:
- **Positive reinforcement**: add a reward → behaviour continues.
- **Negative reinforcement**: remove an aversive stimulus → behaviour continues (e.g. the seatbelt buzzer stops when you buckle up).
- **Positive punishment**: add an aversive stimulus → behaviour stops (e.g. a littering fine).
- **Negative punishment**: remove something pleasant → behaviour stops (e.g. lose break time, move a talker away from friends).
- **Schedules**:
  - fixed ratio (e.g. every 10th coffee free; air miles)
  - variable ratio (slot machines; produces fast, persistent responding and is linked to gambling addiction)
  - fixed interval and variable interval
- **Shaping** (reinforcing successive approximations; the "warmer/colder" class game) [61243].
- **ABC of behaviour**: antecedent, behaviour, consequence [52681].

**Skinner's superstition study** [52681]: pigeons got food every 30 seconds whatever they did. They repeated whatever they happened to be doing (circling, head-tossing), which is accidental reinforcement. It is used to explain superstitions (e.g. the belief that a draught causes colds).

**Why loyalty schemes can fail** [61243]: consistency, schedule, how desirable the reward is, motivation (distance, time limits, perks), and cognitive factors (understanding the scheme, judging whether the reward is worth it).

**Perspective and evaluation** [52681, 64601, 61243]:
- Behaviourism is reductionist and ignores the "black box".
- Humans also weigh expectations and delayed rewards (saving, exercise), so the simple S-R account falls short.
- Bandura showed learning without direct reinforcement.
- Modern view: reinforcement and cognition interact.

**What the examiner wants in B** [52681, 65394]:
- correct terminology
- one strategy (not all four quadrants)
- *why* it would work (e.g. reinforcement generally beats punishment, which brings emotional side effects)

### 3.4 Social learning theory (Bandura)

**Theory** [50766, 61247]: we learn by observing **models** and imitating them, without direct experience. Seeing a model rewarded (**vicarious reinforcement**) makes imitation more likely.

**Four cognitive mediators** [50766, 61247]:
- **Attention**: helped by the model's attractiveness, authority, desirability; hurt by distraction.
- **Retention**: remembering the behaviour; needs familiarity with the vocabulary and a chance to practise soon.
- **Reproduction / "potential"**: being physically or mentally able to do it; needs **self-efficacy**.
- **Motivation**: wanting to do it; based on **outcome expectancies**.

**Model factors** [50766]:
- consistency
- identification (similar age or gender; an in-group bias)
- warmth / liking
- the model's behaviour being rewarded
- Luck-based outcomes are *less* imitated [50766 quiz].

**Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961)** [64551, 50766, 61247]:
- *Aims*: is aggression learned by observation, and are same-sex models imitated more?
- *Four predictions*:
  1. children who watch aggression copy it
  2. children who watch calm models are less aggressive
  3. same-sex models are imitated more
  4. boys imitate aggression more than girls
- *Participants*: **72 children (36 boys, 36 girls), aged 3 to nearly 6** (mean about 52 months) [61247]; described as children of university staff [50766] (the Stanford nursery is not InThinking). One male and one female adult model.
- *Design*:
  - Three IVs: model behaviour (aggressive, non-aggressive, or none as a control), sex of the model, sex of the child. That gives **8 experimental groups plus a control**.
  - Children were **matched** on pre-rated aggression (physical, verbal, object; 5-point scales rated by the experimenter, a teacher and the male model).
  - Inter-rater reliability on 51 children: **r = 0.89**.
- *Procedure*:
  1. **Modelling, 10 min.** In the aggressive condition the model hit Bobo with a mallet, following a script, and shouted things at it; in the non-aggressive condition the model assembled toys quietly.
  2. **Mild aggression arousal.** The child was shown attractive toys, then told they were reserved for other children.
  3. **Test, 20 min** in a room with aggressive and non-aggressive toys and a smaller Bobo. Observers behind a one-way mirror coded behaviour **every 5 s (240 response units)**. Categories: imitative physical, imitative verbal, partial imitation, non-imitative aggression, gun play.
- *Results*:
  - The aggressive-model groups showed much more physical and verbal aggression, including exact imitation.
  - Boys were more aggressive overall.
  - Boys copied the male model more. Girls copied physical aggression more from the male model but verbal aggression more from the female model. Punching Bobo reversed this pattern.
  - Children objected to the female model's aggression as unladylike and praised the male model [64551, 50766].
- *Conclusion*: aggression can be learned by observation. This has implications for violent media. The study did not test whether aggression is also innate [64551].
- *Evaluation / concepts* [64551, 61247, 50766]:
  - **Causality**: a true experiment with a control group and high internal control. But only short-term effects were measured (20 min), there was no follow-up, and it was a lab.
  - **Measurement**: systematic coded observation, r = 0.89. Construct validity is doubtful: hitting an inflatable doll may be play, not aggression. Low ecological validity.
  - **Bias**: children's gender norms shaped imitation. Two observers knew the children (observer bias). Demand characteristics / expectancy effect. An opportunity sample of university-staff children, with small cells.
  - **Responsibility**: consent from young children and parents is questionable; deliberate frustration; no debrief or follow-up; risk that aggression persists. Societal responsibility over media violence and over-simplifying the causes of aggression.
- **Follow-up, Bandura, Ross and Ross (1963)**: filmed or cartoon aggression was also imitated, compared with a no-film control [50766].

**Other SLT evidence** [50766, 61247]:
- **Coates et al. (2019)**: 9–11-year-olds viewed mock Instagram posts of YouTubers with unhealthy food, healthy food or a non-food product.
  - The unhealthy-food group ate **26% more food overall (+91 kcal)** than the control group and **15% more** than the healthy-food group, mainly more unhealthy snacks.
  - Healthy posts had no effect compared with the control.
- **Mirror neurons**:
  - **Gallese et al. (1996)**: macaque premotor neurons fired both when the monkey reached for a peanut and when it watched a researcher do so.
  - **Carr et al. (2003)**: fMRI showed similar brain areas, including the limbic system, when people imitated and when they watched emotional faces. This gives biological support and links to *perspective*.
- **Lowe et al. (2004), "Food Dudes"**: cartoon superhero peer models plus rewards raised children's fruit and vegetable intake. Effects held at a 4-month follow-up and were largest in the lowest eaters. It was quasi-experimental (whole schools; two inner-city London schools) [61247].

**Evaluating SLT** [50766]:
- *Strengths*: robust (explains many behaviours); predictive factors; biological support (mirror neurons).
- *Weaknesses*: hard to test naturally; motivation, self-efficacy and attention are hard to measure; leans to the nurture side and can't explain individual differences alone.

### 3.5 Schema theory (Bartlett; Bransford and Johnson)

**Theory** [50727, 69904, 61239]:
- A **schema** is a mental representation built from experience. Schemas organise knowledge, guide attention and recall, predict what comes next, and guide behaviour. They make information processing economical.
- **Assimilation**: new information fits into an existing schema.
- **Accommodation**: the schema is changed, or a new one created (child calls a cow "doggie", then learns "cow").
- **Disequilibrium** (Piaget): the discomfort when new information doesn't fit.
- Learning = a balance of assimilation and accommodation [50727].
- **Scripts**: event schemas for typical sequences (restaurant, shop, joke). Scripts are culture-specific.
  - Woolworths fires (UK, late 1970s): diners reportedly stayed to pay their bill, following the restaurant script instead of fleeing [50727, 61239].
  - Humour often comes from breaking a script [61239].
- **Schema-congruent** information is processed quickly (System 1 link). **Incongruent** information needs more effort and can be forgotten, distorted, or prompt schema change (System 2 link) [69904, 53406].
- Schema theory explains **memory distortion**, which neither the MSM nor the WMM can [50727].
- **It is a theory, not a cognitive model** [53406, 61238].

**Bartlett (1932), "The War of the Ghosts"** [64632, 53406, 67653]:
- *Aim*: does prior (cultural) knowledge distort recall of an unfamiliar story? Hypothesis: memory is reconstructive and shaped by cultural schemas.
- *Participants*: British participants. The material was a Native American legend with unfamiliar names, ideas and structure.
- *Method*:
  - **Repeated reproduction**: the same person recalls the story many times over minutes to years.
  - **Serial reproduction**: each person retells it to the next, like the telephone game.
- *Results*:
  - There was **no real difference between the two methods** (the null hypothesis was retained [53406]).
  - The story shrank to about **180 words after 6–7 reproductions** [53406].
  - It became more conventional: "hunting seals" became "fishing"; "canoe" became "boat" [53406, 67653].
  - Three distortion patterns:
    - **Levelling**: shortening and omitting details that seem unimportant.
    - **Sharpening**: reordering, adding detail or emotion, turning unfamiliar things into familiar ones.
    - **Rationalisation**: making the story fit British cultural expectations so it "makes sense".
  - The main themes survived [64632].
- *Conclusion*: remembering is active **reconstruction** ("effort after meaning") shaped by schemas. This launched reconstructive memory theory [64632, 64633].
- *Evaluation* [64632, 53406]:
  - Unstandardised procedure (irregular recall intervals; Bartlett scored recall himself), so it is hard to replicate (low reliability) and open to researcher bias.
  - British-only sample.
  - Credit: relatively naturalistic material, and it studied mental processes when behaviourism dominated.
  - Concepts: **bias** (sample, researcher), **change** (memory changes over time), **perspective** (cultural lens shapes recall).

**Bransford and Johnson (1972)** [64633]:
- *Aim*: does *activating* a schema before encoding improve comprehension and recall of an ambiguous passage? Having knowledge is not enough; it must be activated.
- *Participants*: **52** (random allocation, independent measures). **No Topic n = 17; Topic After n = 17; Topic Before n = 18.**
- *Procedure*:
  - Everyone heard the same taped, vague paragraph (it is really about **washing clothes**).
  - The Topic Before group was told the topic first. The Topic After group was told it after hearing the passage. The No Topic group was never told.
  - Participants rated their comprehension on a **1–7 scale** and recalled the passage.
  - Recall was scored against **18 idea units** by **two independent judges**.
- *Results*: the Topic Before group recalled significantly more idea units and rated the passage easier to understand than both other groups. Topic After was no better than No Topic.
  - Mean scores from the original paper, (not InThinking) because the site's table is an image: comprehension about 4.5 for Topic Before vs about 2.1–2.3 for the other groups; recall about 5.8 of 18 vs about 2.7–2.8. Verify before use.
- *Conclusion*: prior knowledge helps only when activated as context **at encoding**.
- *Concepts* [64633]:
  - **causality**: random allocation and a controlled procedure support a causal claim; prior familiarity and attention remain possible confounds
  - **measurement**: idea units plus two judges improve reliability, but judging paraphrases is subjective; comprehension was self-rated
  - **responsibility**: minimal risk; mild deception by omission

**Other schema studies** (recommended alternatives [68593]):
- **Anderson and Pichert (1978)** [64631]:
  - 39 students read a house story as a burglar or a homebuyer (72 ideas: 15 burglar, 13 buyer).
  - Each group recalled more details relevant to its perspective.
  - On a second recall, those who switched perspective recalled **7.1% more** new-perspective information; those who kept the same perspective recalled **2.9% less**.
  - So schemas affect retrieval as well as encoding.
  - Replicated by Borland et al. (1987) with 214 students.
- **Brewer and Treyens (1981)** [64634]:
  - 86 students waited 35 s in an "office".
  - They recalled schema-consistent items and missed odd items (a skull) in free recall.
  - They falsely recognised expected items that weren't there (books).
  - They distorted details (paper moved onto the desk; a trapezoid table recalled as square).
  - 93% did not expect a memory test.
- **Martin and Halverson (1983)** [53406]:
  - 48 children aged 5–6 saw gender-consistent and gender-inconsistent pictures.
  - A week later they distorted the inconsistent ones (a girl with a hammer recalled as a boy).
- **Loftus and Palmer (1974)** [53406]:
  - 150 students saw a crash film; the verb in a question was "smashed", "hit", or there was no question.
  - A week later, "broken glass" (there was none) was reported by **16/50 (32%)** in the smashed group, **7/50 (14%)** in the hit group and **6/50 (12%)** in the control group.
  - This is the misinformation effect.

**Evaluating schema theory** [50727, 61239]:
- *Strengths*:
  - Many experiments (though often artificial, with low ecological validity).
  - **Biological support: Mahon et al. (2009)**. Living and non-living objects are processed in different visual-cortex regions, even in blind people.
  - **Robust**: applied to depression and anxiety therapy, mate selection and health campaigns.
  - Predicts trends in recall.
- *Weaknesses*:
  - **Cohen (1993)**: "schema" is too vague to observe or measure, which challenges construct validity.
  - It predicts group trends, not what an individual will recall.
  - Risk of circular explanation ("they recalled it that way because of their schema").
- Modern rebuttal to Cohen: the medial prefrontal cortex and hippocampus integrate new information with prior knowledge [61239].
- Links to **change / soft determinism** (change schemas, change behaviour) [50727].

### 3.6 Memory basics

[55795, 60919]:
- **Memory** = encoding → retention/storage → retrieval (APA definition).
- Types:
  - **semantic**: facts
  - **episodic**: autobiographical events
  - **procedural**: skills, often unconscious
  - perceptual and emotional memory [60919]
- Ways to study memory: post-mortems, brain-damage case studies, animal research, recall/recognition tests, interviews, fMRI/EEG [60919, 55795].
- Measurement problems: individual differences, self-report, context change, unverifiable autobiographical memories, cost and interpretation of scans [55795].
- **Wang (2001)**: 256 US and Chinese students. Americans' earliest memories were about 6 months earlier and self-focused and emotional; Chinese memories were brief, collective and neutral. This is a cultural factor in memory [55795].

### 3.7 Multi-store model (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968)

**Structure** [50728, 61238]:
- Three stores in sequence: **sensory memory → STM → LTM**. They differ in **encoding, capacity and duration**.
- **Sensory memory**:
  - modality-specific
  - iconic (vision) lasts about **0.2–0.5 s**; echoic (sound) about **2–4 s**
  - **attention** moves information on to STM
- **STM**:
  - capacity **7 ± 2** (Miller, 1956)
  - duration about **15–30 s** [50728] (18–30 s in [61238])
  - without rehearsal, information decays or is **displaced** by new input
- **LTM**:
  - effectively unlimited capacity and duration
  - retrieval brings information back into STM
- **Rehearsal**:
  - **Maintenance**: repetition.
  - **Elaborative**: linking to existing knowledge; deeper, and more effective.
- **Cowan (2010)**: when people don't know how long a list will be (running span), STM holds only **3–5 items**. Miller's rising-length task invited strategies, which lowers its ecological validity [50728].

**Serial position effect** [50728, 61854, 64637]:
- **Primacy**: early items get rehearsed into LTM.
- **Recency**: late items are still in STM.
- The middle of the curve (the asymptote) = items neither rehearsed nor still held.

**Glanzer and Cunitz (1966)** [64637, 50728]:
- *Aim*: what happens to the serial position curve if rehearsal of the last items is blocked?
- *Participants*: **46 enlisted army men**, repeated measures, tested individually; 3 practice lists.
- *Procedure*:
  - **15 lists of 15** common one-syllable words, shown 1 s each with 2 s gaps, also read aloud; list order randomised.
  - Then either immediate free recall, or counting from a given digit for **10 s** or **30 s** before recall.
- *Results*: immediate recall showed primacy and recency. **10 s delay: recency sharply reduced. 30 s delay: "no trace" of recency.** Primacy was unaffected.
- *Conclusion*: supports separate STM and LTM. Early words were already in LTM through rehearsal; late words were displaced from STM.
- *Evaluation* [64637, 50728]:
  - true experiment with high control (causality)
  - low mundane / ecological validity (lists of random words)
  - free recall under a time limit may underestimate memory
  - repeated measures controls participant variables but risks boredom
  - soldiers may not have felt free to withdraw

**Patient HM (Milner, 1966 and later; Corkin MRI 1992/2003)** [64939, 50728, 61238]:
- *Background*: Henry Molaison, born 1926. Epilepsy followed a head injury at age 7. In **1953, at 27**, Scoville removed tissue from both medial temporal lobes, including most of the hippocampi and parts of the amygdala.
- *Method*: longitudinal case study with **method triangulation**: IQ tests (above average), observation, interviews, memory tasks, mirror drawing, later MRI.
- *Findings*:
  - Severe **anterograde amnesia**: could not form new episodic or semantic LTM.
  - Retrograde loss at first covered about 12 years, later shrinking to about 1 year.
  - **STM / working memory intact**: he held the number **584 for 15 minutes** by constant rehearsal, then lost it once distracted.
  - He learned **mirror drawing** (procedural memory) without remembering ever having done it.
  - He could draw his new home's layout (spatial memory).
  - He retained some post-surgery facts (e.g. JFK's assassination).
- *Conclusions*: the hippocampus transfers STM to LTM but is not the permanent store. STM and LTM are separate (supports the MSM). Implicit/procedural memory is a separate system (**challenges the MSM's single LTM store**).
- *Concepts* [64939]:
  - bias: long researcher relationship; n = 1
  - causality: correlational; damage extent unknown until MRI; retrospective baseline data
  - measurement: several methods; early observation was subjective
  - responsibility: could HM consent? proxy consent; confidentiality kept by using initials; decades of testing
- **Clive Wearing** (herpes encephalitis) is a comparison case: anterograde plus severe retrograde amnesia [61238].

**Evaluating the MSM** [50728, 61238, 52683]:
- *Strengths*: experimental support (serial position, G&C) plus biological support (HM); historically foundational.
- *Weaknesses*:
  - oversimplified: stores treated as independent and linear
  - doesn't explain distortion, emotion, learning with little rehearsal, or rehearsal that fails to transfer
  - **Logie (1999)**: STM is a workstation that interacts with LTM, not a gateway
  - HM's procedural learning shows several LTM stores
  - LOP challenges the idea that rehearsal alone matters
- "All models are wrong, but some are useful" (George Box) [61238].

### 3.8 Working memory model (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974)

**Structure** [50738, 61882]: STM is several components, not one store. The evidence comes from **dual-task** studies: two auditory tasks interfere with each other; an auditory plus a visual task do not.
- **Central executive**:
  - attention controller
  - allocates input to the subsystems; focuses, divides and switches attention
  - links to LTM
  - limited capacity, modality-free
- **Phonological loop**:
  - **phonological store** ("inner ear"; traces last about **1.5–2 s** without rehearsal)
  - **articulatory control process** ("inner voice"; subvocal rehearsal)
- **Visuospatial sketchpad** ("inner eye"): visual and spatial information.
- **Episodic buffer** (added later by Baddeley; the year 2000 is not InThinking): integrates information from the subsystems and LTM; limited capacity; linked to conscious awareness.

**Landry and Bartling (2011)** [50738, 61882]:
- *Aim*: does articulatory suppression impair serial recall of phonologically dissimilar letters?
- *Participants*: **34 undergraduate psychology students**, independent samples (17 per group).
- *Procedure*:
  - **10 lists of 7 letters** drawn from F, K, L, M, R, X, Q (no vowels; no similar-sounding letters).
  - Each list shown for 5 s, then a 5 s wait, then written recall in order.
  - The experimental group repeated "1, 2" at two numbers per second from the moment of presentation until writing.
- *Results*: mean correct recall **76% in the control group vs 45% in the suppression group**, **p ≤ 0.01**.
- *Conclusion*: suppression occupies the phonological loop and blocks rehearsal. This supports a separate verbal subsystem.
- *Evaluation*: independent samples avoids order effects, but 17 per group means individual differences may matter. Artificial lab task [61882].

**KF, Warrington and Shallice (1970/1972/1974)** [50738]:
- A motorcycle accident left LTM intact but auditory-verbal STM impaired.
- He was poor with spoken digits and words but fine when they were shown visually, and fine with non-verbal sounds (meowing, ringing phones).
- So there are separate verbal and visual short-term stores. This supports the WMM and challenges the unitary STM of the MSM.

**Evaluating the WMM** [50738, 61882]:
- *Strengths*: dual-task evidence; brain scans show different areas for verbal vs visual tasks; brain-damage cases; explains when multitasking works (music + drawing) and when it fails (podcast + conversation; phone use while driving).
- *Weaknesses*: the central executive is vague and its capacity can't be measured separately; how the components interact is unclear; neuroimaging localisation is inconsistent; says little about LTM; doesn't explain distortion or emotion.
- *Measurement concerns*: amount of rehearsal; the capacity and function of the central executive and episodic buffer [50738].

**Related strategy: dual coding (Paivio)** [61882]: verbal plus visual encoding creates two traces. It rests on the WMM's separate channels.

### 3.9 Levels of processing (Craik and Lockhart, 1972)

InThinking coverage is thin: only a teacher activity page [25399], plus a mention on the MSM lesson page as research that **challenges the MSM** [61238].
- *Theory* [25399]: how long a memory lasts depends on **depth of processing**, not on which store it sits in.
  - **Shallow**: structural (what a word looks like, e.g. capital letters) or phonological (sound).
  - **Deep**: semantic (meaning, links to prior knowledge, imagery). Deep processing gives better recall.
- *Contrast with the MSM*: the MSM treats rehearsal and transfer as one general process and doesn't distinguish types of processing [25399]. The MSM quiz notes that "rehearsal" has several types, as LOP shows, which makes rehearsal hard to standardise as a construct [61238].
- *Limitation*: **depth can't be measured independently**; you can't check that the "shallow" group didn't secretly form images. This is a risk of circularity [25399].
- *Class demo* [25399]: 20 sentences, one group processing shallowly and one with imagery, then cued recall scored out of 40. Typical scores: **deep about 25–30 vs shallow about 7–15**.
- **Craik and Tulving (1975)**: *no InThinking page exists* (a site search found only comment mentions). (not InThinking)
  - Participants answered structural (case), phonemic (rhyme) or semantic (fits a sentence) questions about words, then had a surprise recognition test.
  - Recognition rose with depth, highest for semantic "yes" words, roughly 15–20% for structural vs 60–80% for semantic.
  - Verify figures against the source before putting them in the game.

### 3.10 Strategies to improve memory (Section C only)

[55797, 64616, c55797]:
- **Method of loci**:
  - Place items along a familiar route; "walk" it to recall. Uses strong spatial memory and elaborative encoding.
  - **Dresler et al. (2017)**:
    - 23 memory athletes vs 51 IQ-matched controls; controls were randomised to 6 weeks of loci training, working-memory training, or none.
    - Loci trainees **more than doubled** recall of **72 words**. Their brain connectivity came to resemble the athletes'.
    - The gain persisted at **4 months**; the other groups showed none.
- **Spaced repetition**:
  - reviews at growing intervals (Anki, Duolingo)
  - **Gilbert et al. (2023)**: 131 first-year medical students, 78 of whom used Anki; heavier use went with **12.9% higher** exam scores (**p = 0.003**)
  - quasi-experimental, self-selected, motivated sample
- **Indigenous memory**:
  - **Kearins (1981)**: Aboriginal Australian children outperformed white Australian children at recalling object locations.
  - **Reser (2021)**: Aboriginal place-based story methods improved medical students' recall.
  - Perspective links [64616].
- Also accepted: chunking, dual coding, sleep and exercise. The cognitive interview is more about retrieval, so it is doubtful [c55797].

### 3.11 PNT topics (possibly not taught): brief facts

- **Cognitive load theory** [52682, 72284, 66860]:
  - Working memory is limited; overload hurts learning.
  - **Intrinsic** = the task's inherent difficulty. **Extraneous** = distraction or poor presentation. **Germane** = effort that builds schemas; lower when prior knowledge helps.
  - **Sana et al. (2013)**: 44 students; multitasking on laptops, and even sitting in view of others' laptops, lowered lecture comprehension.
  - **Mani et al. (2013)**: poorer participants scored lower on Raven's matrices when made to think about a costly (~$1500) car repair. In the field, 464 Indian sugarcane farmers scored **4.35 pre-harvest vs 5.45 post-harvest** on Raven's; Stroop **146 s vs 131 s**; p < 0.001.
- **Dual process theory** [72109, 64925]:
  - **System 1**: fast, automatic, intuitive, heuristic, low effort, error-prone.
  - **System 2**: slow, effortful, analytic.
  - Examples: the Cognitive Reflection Test bat-and-ball problem (intuitive answer 10¢, correct answer 5¢); driving becoming automatic.
  - Studies: Wason (1968), Griggs and Cox (1982) [50729].
- **Anchoring bias** [72119]: the first value acts as a starting point, and adjustment away from it is too small. **Tversky and Kahneman (1974)**: 8×7×…×1 was estimated much higher than 1×2×…×8. Strack and Mussweiler (1997); Englich and Mussweiler (2001) (judges' sentencing) [64925, 50732].
- **Confirmation bias** [72120]: we seek, weight and remember evidence that fits what we already believe and explain away evidence against it. Wason's 2-4-6 task; Lord et al. (1979) [50732].

**Exam technique**
1. **Listing every variant** (all four OC quadrants, several examples) wastes time. **Only the first example is marked** [65434, 64925, 67655, 72284].
2. **Describe vs explain**: a description earns about 2/4 on an "explain" question [70442, 72119 sample 2].
3. **Answering a different theory**: writing about DPT when asked about anchoring or confirmation bias scores only 1–2/4 [72119, 72120].
4. **Section B labelling without explaining**, and not saying *why* (e.g. why she is prone to confirmation bias): "command term not met" [71976, 67653, 67655].
   - Starting B without first explaining the theory [67655].
   - Evaluating in B [71976].
   - Chasing too many links [71976].
5. **Drawing the model** without describing its components and interactions [52683].
6. **Section C**:
   - Discussing only the concept, e.g. bias, with no actual content (an environmental effect on memory): capped at about 9 [67141].
   - Introducing the content and then dropping it [67141 sample 2].
   - A conclusion that brings in new ideas [67141].
   - Links saved for the end instead of running throughout [65928].
   - Ethics or generalisability in a *perspective* essay: irrelevant, no credit [65928].
   - Too much study methodology instead of perspective [67173].

**Content errors**
7. **Mixing MSM and WMM vocabulary**: "working memory" and "central executive" used inside the MSM [72285].
8. Calling sensory memory "selective memory" [67173].
9. **Schema theory is not a cognitive model** and can't answer a model question [61238, 53406].
10. Students often write about an *application* of schema theory (reconstructive memory) instead of explaining the theory itself [69904].
11. **Bias vs perspective confusion**: a single-approach study is not "biased" [55777, 64652].
12. **Wrong validity term**: a sampling problem affects **external/population validity, not ecological validity** [66756, 67655]. A child's age difference is a confounding variable, not "participant bias" [67655].
13. **Vague absolutes**: don't write "internal validity was high/low", "causality was low", "the study was unethical" or "biased" [65934, 65931].
14. Don't say "accurate" about a psychology study; say reliable or valid [50766 quiz].
15. "Correlation is not causation" used as a slogan; causality comes in degrees and types (linear, domino, cyclical) [50714].
16. Cognitive load: germane load is often left undeveloped or confused [72284, 67653, 66860 sample 1]. Defining cognitive load as "using lots of technology" is wrong; it is mental effort in working memory [66860 sample 2].
17. SLT: "people just copy others" is too thin; name ARRM plus vicarious reinforcement [71997]. In-group similarity to the model helps but is *not required* [50766 quiz].
18. Pavlov's stimulus was a **metronome**, not a bell. It isn't penalised, but know it [65434, 52680].
19. Negative reinforcement is *removing* something unpleasant to *increase* behaviour. It is not punishment [52681].

20. **Improving a cognitive process is Section C only** [c55797]. Environment and cognition, and culture and cognition, are also Section C only; Sections A/B use only the approach tables [c55797, c59706].
21. Only one model of memory is needed; "compare two models" is gone [c61238].
